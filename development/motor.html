Build guide G.01 — actuators · status: draft

Building the actuator

Every joint is driven by the same kind of unit: a brushless motor, a 3D-printed cycloidal reducer, a magnetic encoder and a small driver on a CAN bus. This guide follows the Berkeley Humanoid Lite design and maps it onto the Unitree G1’s 29 joints.

§ 03

What’s inside

The motor is a standard brushless drone motor. Its output goes into a 3D-printed cycloidal reducer, which spreads the load over many teeth and copes better with a desktop printer’s limited accuracy than planetary gears do. The original Berkeley Humanoid used 9:1 planetary actuators; the printable Lite version swaps in the cycloidal design, and that is the one we follow.

§ 05

Build steps

— 05 / 06 —

  1. Print the parts

    Print the housing, cycloidal disc, input shaft and output shaft in PLA on a desktop FDM printer. Berkeley sized every part to fit a 200 × 200 × 200 mm build volume. Our own tested print settings will be added here.

  2. Embed the brass standoff

    A brass hex standoff is pressed into the input shaft. It stiffens the shaft and carries torque from the motor to the cycloidal discs.

  3. Fit the bearings

    Press the bearings into the housing and output shaft. The 6512 design is sized around the common 6811ZZ ball bearing.

  4. Assemble the cycloidal stage

    Stack the cycloidal disc on the eccentric input shaft and seat it against the pins in the housing. It should turn smoothly by hand before the motor goes on.

  5. Mount the motor and encoder

    Fix the motor to the input shaft, then mount the AS5600 magnetic encoder and its magnet as shown in the CAD.

  6. Clamp with through-screws

    Long through-screws pull the layers together so the printed parts aren’t relying on layer adhesion alone.

  7. Wire the driver

    Connect motor phases, encoder, power and CAN to the B-G431B-ESC1. Each CAN bus runs at 1 Mbps and Berkeley’s design supports up to 64 devices on one bus, so give every joint its own ID.

  8. Calibrate and test

    Set the zero position, then check smooth rotation, torque response and backlash before the actuator goes into the robot. Test on a bench power supply, not the battery.

Safety: motors can pinch fingers and LiPo batteries can start fires if shorted or damaged. Keep clear of moving parts, use a bench supply while testing, and never leave a charging battery unattended.

Sizing for the G1

The G1’s torque limits fall into five tiers. Berkeley’s printed actuators come in two sizes, and the paper doesn’t give their peak torque, so check their repository before committing a joint to one. The hips and knees are the hard part: the G1’s knee limit is well above the 81.1 N·m peak of the largest original Berkeley Humanoid actuator.
