Learning to balance

Before the printed robot stands up, a simulated one has to. This article explains how we use reinforcement learning to make a simulated G1 copy a person’s pose without falling over, what went wrong first, and how the same simulation can help us choose 3D-print infill for every part.

§ 01

Why learn it in simulation

— 01 / 08 —

There is no neat formula for keeping a 29-joint humanoid upright while it reaches for something. Reinforcement learning (RL) side-steps the formula. A small neural network, the policy, looks at the robot fifty times a second and decides where every joint should move next. After each step a reward scores how well that went, and a learning algorithm (here PPO) nudges the network towards whatever earned more reward.

That needs millions of attempts and a lot of falling over, which is fine for a simulated robot and not for a printed one. So the learning happens in MuJoCo, a physics simulator, using a model of the Unitree G1. OpenG1 copies the G1’s proportions and joint layout, which is what lets this work carry over.

The task we chose is pose copying. Give the robot target positions for a person’s hands and feet, and optionally elbows, knees, hips and shoulders, and it has to put its own limbs there while staying balanced. Later those targets will come from a camera-based body tracker, which turns the robot into something you can drive by moving.

§ 02

First attempt: borrowed legs

— 02 / 08 —

Unitree publishes a pretrained walking policy for the G1. Our first plan was to let it run the legs, holding the robot still, and train a second policy for the waist and arms to reach the hand targets.

It never learned. The run’s progress.csv shows episodes ending after about 45 of their 500 steps for the whole 1.5 million steps, so the robot fell within a second every time. The reward curve was noisy because it mostly measured when the robot fell: each fall cost −10, against about +0.5 per step it stayed up (the average in that run).

The cause was not in the learning at all. Running the walking policy on its own, with no arm movement, showed it couldn’t keep the 29-joint robot standing either:

SetupStays up for
12-joint G1 model (what the walking policy was trained on)10 s (full episode)
29-joint G1 model, arms perfectly still1.6 s
29-joint model, arms exploring randomly (our training)0.4 – 1.3 s
29-joint model with joint armature restored10 s, even with random arm motion

Unitree’s 29-joint model file (g1_29dof.xml) sets no armature on any joint, while the 12-joint model the walking policy learned on uses 0.01 kg·m². Armature is the motor’s rotor inertia as felt at the joint: the rotor inertia multiplied by the gear ratio squared (explained here). It is small, but a gearbox makes it matter, and a PD loop tuned with it behaves very differently without it. Setting the 12-joint model’s 0.01 kg·m² on every joint fixed standing outright. Our environment now uses per-motor values from BeyondMimic’s G1 config, between 0.0036 and 0.025 kg·m², which also stand for the full 10 s.

LessonA reward can’t fix a simulation that is wrong about the hardware. When OpenG1’s printed cycloidal actuators exist, their armature (rotor inertia × ratio²) and every link’s mass have to go into the model before a policy trained on it can work on the real robot. § 06 is about the mass part.

§ 03

One policy, whole body

— 03 / 08 —

Even standing, a frozen walking policy can’t hold a foot where you ask; it only knows how to step. So we replaced it with a single policy that drives all 23 joints of the legs, waist and arms (wrists are held still for now). It follows the recipe used in published motion-imitation work such as DeepMimic and H2O, scaled down to train on one workstation.

23 joints · 50 Hz

  1. Make reference motions

    The policy needs lots of poses that are physically possible. A generator builds them with whole-body inverse kinematics, keeping the centre of mass over the feet that are on the ground, checking that limbs don’t collide and limiting joint speed to 3 rad/s. The library has 120 clips, 43 minutes in all, in four kinds: arm and waist moves, squats and leans with both feet planted, balancing on one leg, and stepping.

  2. Start in the middle

    Every episode starts at a random moment of a random clip, with the robot placed exactly in that pose. Hard moments get practised as often as easy ones.

  3. Learn corrections, not poses

    The policy’s output is a small correction added to the reference joint angles. On top of that, a gravity feed-forward term adds the torque needed to hold the reference pose up, computed from the model. Without it the untrained robot sags by 4.7 cm of pose error in the first 0.2 s; with it, 1.4 cm.

  4. Stop early

    An episode ends when the robot falls (pelvis tilted past 45° or 25 cm too low) or drifts too far from the pose (mean error over 30 cm). No time is wasted flailing on the floor.

  5. Score each step

    The reward is between roughly 0 and 1 per step, with no big fall penalty. Ending early is the penalty: the robot loses every reward it would have earned.

From a body tracker to joint angles

A tracker gives positions, not joint angles. A retargeting step solves inverse kinematics on the G1 model to turn tracker points into a full robot pose in about 2 ms per frame. With 12 points it reproduces them to within a few millimetres. With only hands and feet it still hits those four, but has to guess the elbows and knees (about 8 cm off on average), which is why we’ll use more tracker points when we can.

§ 04

It tracked, then it fell

— 04 / 08 —

The first whole-body training run did learn to match poses, and the reward rose. Then it fell again: the robot copied the pose well and tipped over later in the episode. Three things were behind it:

  • three quarters of the reward was pose accuracy, so the policy happily traded balance for accuracy;
  • the balance terms (pelvis height and tilt) only reacted once a fall was already under way;
  • PPO was set to care about roughly the next 2 seconds, so a slow drift towards a fall cost almost nothing at the moment it started.

Balance matters more than exact accuracy: a robot on the floor copies nothing. So we rebuilt the reward around it.

Seeing a fall coming

The most useful new term is the capture point. Take the robot’s centre of mass, and add its horizontal speed divided by ω = √(g / h), where h is the height of the centre of mass. That is where the robot would have to put a foot to come to a stop. If it lies inside the area covered by the feet on the ground (the support polygon), the robot can recover without stepping. If it lies outside, a fall has already started, even though nothing looks wrong yet. We work out the support polygon from the four contact points under each sole, and the reward pays full marks while the capture point stays at least 2 cm inside it.

To check it really warns early, we let the untrained robot fall 118 times and lined the falls up by the moment each one ended:

A critic that sees more. PPO trains two networks: the policy (actor) and a critic that predicts how much reward is coming. The critic now also sees the robot’s true velocity, centre of mass, capture-point margin, foot contacts, floor friction and any push, so it can predict a fall. The actor still sees only what the real robot can measure, so it stays deployable.

A curriculum. Training starts with motions where both feet stay planted. Balancing on one leg unlocks once the robot survives 80 % of those episodes, and stepping after that. Easy motions keep being practised so they aren’t forgotten.

Stronger ankles. The ankle’s position loop has a stiffness of 40 N·m per radian (the gain in Unitree’s G1 config), so a 0.4 rad correction made at most 16 N·m. For the model’s 35 kg that moves the pressure under the foot by about 4.6 cm, a quarter of the 17 cm between the front and back contact points in g1_29dof.xml. Raising the range to 0.7 rad allows about 8 cm.

§ 05 shows what these changes did over the whole first stage of training, 60 million steps.

§ 05

Watching it learn

A training run takes many hours, and the reward curve alone doesn’t say whether the robot is getting better at balancing. Every run therefore records the same fixed test episodes as it goes: survival per motion kind, pose error, and a short video, every 5 % of the run. A model backup is saved every 10 %, and a small script turns any backup into a GIF like Fig. 3.

Stage 1: what the log says

The first stage of training has finished: 60 million steps, 10.4 hours with 16 simulated robots. Fig. 4 is drawn straight from that run’s progress.csv. This page loads the file and plots it, and the numbers in this section are read from it too.

Feet-planted motions first. The untrained policy stayed up for 13 % of a test episode. One-leg balance joined training at 1.9 million steps, once both feet-planted kinds survived 80 % of their training episodes, and by the second test (3 million steps) the robot stayed up through every arm-and-waist and whole-body test episode.

One leg is hard. Its test score went from 13 % to 93 %, but jumped around on the way. There are only two test episodes per kind, so one early end moves a kind’s score by up to 50 points.

Stepping, partly for free. Stepping only joined training at 46.0 million steps, after one-leg balance passed 80 %. From 12 million steps on, its test score was already above 70 % in all but one test, probably because lifting a foot and putting it back is also part of the one-leg motions. From 51 million steps on, the robot survived every stepping test.

Falls became rare. In the first update 98 % of training episodes ended early (34 % with a fall, the rest by drifting more than 30 cm from the pose). By the end, 4.5 % ended with a fall and 16 % by losing the pose, so what is left is mostly accuracy. The capture point moved from −9.6 cm, outside the feet, to +4.6 cm, inside: the margin the balance reward in § 04 pays for.

Feet are the hardest to place. Pose error over the 12 keypoints fell from 8.8 to 5.6 cm in training. The feet stay furthest off (9.7 cm, against 6.2 cm for the hands), probably because they travel furthest in the one-leg and stepping motions.

The last test wasn’t the best. The best score was 98 %, at 57.0 million steps. The final test fell to 89 % because whole-body survival dropped to 61 %, which with two episodes per kind can be a single early end. Stage 2 comes next: 40 million more steps with pushes, varied floor friction and sensor noise.

§ 06

Mass, infill and the model

— 06 / 08 —

Everything above uses the masses in g1_29dof.xml, the G1 as Unitree modelled it. OpenG1’s links will be printed, and a printed part’s mass depends on its infill, walls and material. That is a choice we get to make part by part, and the simulator can show what each choice does to the joints and to balance before anything is printed. All numbers in this section were computed from that file; § 07 lists where each one comes from.

Holding torque scales directly with the mass being held: make the upper body 20 % heavier and the waist pitch needs 20 % more just to stand in these poses, before any balancing on top. The waist is already the tightest joint, because it carries the whole upper body, so a lighter torso buys more margin there than mass saved anywhere else.

Where mass sits matters for balance

Standing, the model’s centre of mass is 7.1 cm behind the front contact points of its feet. Raising both arms straight out in front moves it forward. Here we scaled the arm masses in the model to see how much that depends on how heavy the arms are:

Mass far from the hips (hands, forearms, shins, feet) shifts the balance point more, and takes more torque to swing, than the same mass near the pelvis. A gram saved at the end of a limb is worth more than a gram saved in the middle.

When the model’s mass is wrong

The gravity feed-forward from § 03, and the policy itself, are built on the model’s masses. To see what a wrong mass does, we made the simulated robot lighter or heavier than the model the controller believes in, and measured the untrained robot’s pose error after 0.2 s (average of 40 episodes):

The feed-forward cuts the error from 4.7 to 1.4 cm. A 30 % mass error gives back up to 0.7 cm of that, and updating the model wins it all back. The trained policy has the same problem in a less visible way: its corrections were learned for one mass. Two fixes, used together: put measured masses in the model, and train with masses that vary a little every episode, so one policy copes with the differences between prints.

What would printed parts weigh?

We don’t have OpenG1’s printed masses yet. Until we do, we can estimate them from the G1’s own link shapes. The meshes that g1_29dof.xml uses are closed surfaces, so their volume and surface area can be measured exactly. A printed part is a solid skin (walls, top and bottom layers) around a partly hollow core, and infill only changes the core:

m ≈ ρ · ( Vskin + f · Vcore ) + mhardware ρ: material density, PLA 1.24 g/cm³ (Prusament PLA, ISO 1183) or PETG 1.27 g/cm³ (Prusament PETG, ISO 1183) · f: infill fraction · Vskin ≈ surface area × skin thickness; we assume 1.2 mm (3 walls of 0.4 mm, and about the same top and bottom) · Vcore: the rest of the volume · mhardware: motors, bearings, screws, battery and electronics, which infill doesn’t change and which this estimate leaves out.

Printed in PLA at 15–40 % infill, plastic in the G1’s shapes would weigh 6.6–10.9 kg; PETG adds about 2 %. The G1 model weighs 35.1 kg, but its links aren’t printed plastic, so this isn’t a like-for-like comparison. What it does show is that at these infills the plastic comes to 19–31 % of the G1 model’s mass. Actuators, battery and electronics come on top of it, and need weighing and putting into the model just as much as the prints.

Two limits on this estimate. OpenG1’s CAD won’t match the G1’s shapes exactly: parts will be hollowed around actuators and split to fit a print bed. And the skin thickness is our assumption, not a measurement. Once there are real parts, use the slicer’s filament estimate for each one, and better still a kitchen scale. Lower infill is also weaker, so load paths (actuator mounts, joint housings, screw bosses) need extra walls or local dense infill even where the rest of a part is light.

Putting the numbers into the simulation

MuJoCo stores each link’s mass and rotational inertia. To give a link its real mass, set the mass and scale the inertia by the same ratio (same shape, new mass). If heavy hardware sits off-centre on a link, take the link’s centre of mass from CAD too. Change both the physics model and the model the gravity feed-forward uses:

pythontested on the env from § 03
import mujoco
from G1_tele.envs.pose_tracking import PoseTrackingEnv

def set_link_mass(model, body, mass_kg):
    """Give a link a new mass, keeping the shape of its inertia."""
    b = model.body(body).id
    model.body_inertia[b] *= mass_kg / model.body_mass[b]
    model.body_mass[b] = mass_kg

env = PoseTrackingEnv()
# the physics model, and the model behind the gravity feed-forward
for m, d in ((env.base.m, env.base.d), (env.kin.m, env.kin.d)):
    set_link_mass(m, "left_knee_link", 1.45)    # example value: slicer estimate + weighed hardware
    set_link_mass(m, "right_knee_link", 1.45)
    mujoco.mj_setConst(m, d)                    # refresh values MuJoCo derives from mass

For training, a few lines in the episode reset give every episode a slightly different print, so the policy can’t rely on one exact mass:

pythonsketch: not in the env yet
# in PoseTrackingEnv.reset(); mass0 / inertia0 saved once at start-up
s = self.np_random.uniform(0.85, 1.15, size=m.nbody)    # ±15 % per link
m.body_mass[:] = self.mass0 * s
m.body_inertia[:] = self.inertia0 * s[:, None]
mujoco.mj_setConst(m, d)

Choosing infill with the simulator

Put together, this gives a loop for choosing infill part by part, with the simulator in the middle:

  1. Pick a first guess

    Light infill where parts are mostly shape (covers, forearm and shin shells), dense infill and extra walls where loads go (actuator mounts, joint housings). Keep the ends of the limbs as light as strength allows.

  2. Get the masses

    Slice each part and note its filament estimate; weigh the hardware that bolts to it. Add them per link.

  3. Update the model

    Write the masses into the model as above, in both the physics model and the gravity feed-forward model.

  4. Check the torque margins

    Run the motion library through the holding-torque check and compare every joint with its actuator’s limit. Watch the waist pitch first: it is at 66 % with the G1 model’s masses.

  5. Fine-tune the policy

    Continue training from the existing policy on the new masses, with ±15 % mass variation. If survival per motion kind holds up, print; if not, go back to step 1 and take mass off where it costs the most.
